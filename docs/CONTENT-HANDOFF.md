## What the site mirrors

The site prepares students for the real CompTIA exam, so it mirrors **the exam**, not my course materials. This decides every choice you make:

1. **The CompTIA objectives and the evidence of what the exam asks decide what is covered and how much.** The evidence: the exam's keyed practice exam (the closest stand-in for the real exam: the Professor Messer practice exams for A+ Core 1, the practice exam book for Network+), the troubleshooting items in Domain 5 that lean on this unit's facts, each objective's verb ("Given a scenario" is asked as scenarios and performance-based questions), and the domain weights.
2. **The CertMaster reading decides every answer,** and every question cites the section that answers it.
3. **The CertMaster pool and the practice exam items** are what each question is modeled on.
4. **My materials** (unit study guides with keys, review decks, lesson slides, Blookets) are built from the CertMaster platform. They are good, but they are not the last word. They corroborate keys and terms, and they fill a gap only where an exam-weighted fact has no pool or practice exam item. They never add weight, and they never override the reading or the objectives.
5. **Questions are presented the way the exam presents them:** short scenarios, "BEST", "MOST likely", "FIRST", and real distractors from the same unit.
6. **Flashcards cover only what is testable.** In my words: "The flashcards should really only focus what is testable. I dont want to confuse students." A term gets a card only when it passes test A, B, or C in rule 6: the objectives name it, or name a group it is one kind of without listing the group's members, or a keyed practice exam item asks about it or has it as its correct answer. A term that only the reading, the pool, or my materials use gets no card, however true or clear the card would be.

## Rules I will hold you to

The guide has the full list. These are the ones that sink a unit:

1. **A cousin, not a copy.** Every question is modeled on one source item and names it in `origin.basedOn`, numbered the way the guide says. Keep the objective, the one distinction it tests, and whether it has one answer or asks for two or three. Change the scenario, the wording, and the option text. The option count follows the guide: 4 for one answer, 5 for choose two or three (6 only when the source item had 6 or more).
2. **The reading decides the answer.** Key every question from the section you cite. Where the reading and a source item disagree (or my key), the reading wins, and you tell me. Where the reading contradicts itself or is plainly wrong, key nothing to that point: add it to `docs/CONTENT-NOTES.md` and tell me.
3. **An item belongs to the unit whose reading answers it.** Every question and card you write cites only sections of your unit (the card tagging in step 9 is the one exception). A command line that a published question or command card already asks may be asked once more by typing (the guide, "A command"): that is not asking the fact again, and it is not a second use of its source item.
4. **Tag as specifically as you can:** the objective and its best-fitting bullet (`3.4` and `3.4.c`), and the reading section (`3.2.6`). Book numbers and CompTIA numbers overlap by accident: `units` and `reference.section` take book numbers, `objectives` takes CompTIA numbers (Network+ lesson 5.5 is not objective 5.5). Bullet ids are this site's letters (`1.4.a`), never the numbers B.1.3 prints on its lines. Never borrow a bullet that does not fit.
5. **Leave `reference.quote` empty.** I add the quotes. Cite the section that teaches the key, not one that only names the topic or teaches a distractor. Give me a good `reference.find`: three to six words from the sentence that gives the key (for a worked answer, the sentence of its rule), taken from one line of the section's `pdftotext -layout` text and inside that one sentence, never across a page break, a hyphenated word, or a table's columns. In a table, the phrase stays inside one cell: the text puts a row's cells on one line, but Ctrl+F in the PDF does not find a phrase that runs from one cell into the next.
6. **Flashcards are only for what the exam tests, so students are not confused.** Judge each term against the objectives your unit's lessons list in `units.json`. A term gets a card only if it passes one of these three tests, and only if a section of your unit teaches it (explains it, not just names it):
   - **A. Named.** The objective's title, one of its bullets, or a detail under a bullet (at any depth) names the term, or names the exact thing the card defines.
   - **B. One kind of a named group.** An objective, bullet, or detail names a group without listing its members (iterations, categories, standards, types, configurations, options), and the term is one of the kinds your unit's reading lists for it (DDR4, Cat 6, 802.11ac). Where the objectives list the members themselves (3.4 lists its DNS record types: A, AAAA, CNAME, MX, text, NS, and pointer), only those pass, by test A. A mode, part, feature, or step inside one kind is not a kind.
   - **C. Keyed by the practice exam.** An item of the exam's keyed practice exam that is tagged to one of those objectives asks about the term (its question turns on what the term is or does), or has the term, or the exact thing the card defines, as its correct answer. A wrong choice, an explanation, or a term the item only mentions in its scenario or in a command's output while it asks about something else does not count (the DUID in the ipconfig /all output of "Practice Tests 5 Q97", which asks about the default gateway). In the Network+ book's Chapters 6 and 7, which carry no objective, the bullet the item asks about decides.

   Nothing else gets a card: not a term only the reading, the pool, or my materials use, however true or clear the card would be, and not a part, tool, cause, or fix that a named symptom or task turns on, unless it passes a test itself (otherwise that fact goes on the named card's back). A back gives what the term is or does and the fact that tells it apart from the other terms in its bullet; any other number, part, menu path, brand or product name, format, or command syntax goes on the back only when it is that fact or a practice exam item turns on it (Unit 6.0's slips: DHCP's half-lease renewal, a port its bullet does not list, "ships with BIND", and a command's syntax template). A back never contains its own term, the term's spelled-out name, or the command it defines: in Definition first mode the back is the question, so that back gives the answer away. Where a section gives a protocol only by its name and port, the back is "The protocol that uses well-known port 22." No brief you fill in may add an exception to this rule. Cards also follow "What makes a good flashcard" in the guide: never two cards for one term in an exam, and every card is fact-checked against its section, and so is every card rewritten after a check.
7. **Wording:** CompTIA voice, plain enough for a 10th grader meeting this for the first time, no em dashes, no "all of the above", and never "(Select TWO.)" in a stem.
8. **A published pack is fixed.** Once a pack is pushed to GitHub, or I have added quotes or set `"reviewed": true`, its ids never change (students' saved decks and tests point at them).
